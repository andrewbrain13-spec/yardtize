# Yardtize — Business & Build Plan (v1)

*Prepared August 3, 2026 · Research-backed planning document · Kansas City pilot market*

---

## 1. The One-Page Summary

**The idea:** A two-sided marketplace where homeowners lease yard space to businesses for advertising signs. Homeowners list their yard on a map, traffic data (AADT) sets the value, businesses browse and book, the platform handles the lease, payments, and the relationship. Yardtize takes a brokerage fee.

**What the research says:** The core mechanics are buildable — the traffic data is public and excellent in Missouri, the marketplace playbook (Peerspace, Sniffspot, Neighbor) is well understood, and the one named competitor (Grass Spaces) is early and weak. But two hard problems shape everything:

1. **Sign ordinances.** Most KC-metro cities prohibit or tightly restrict "off-premise" commercial signs in residential zones, and the Supreme Court blessed exactly those bans in 2022. This is not a permitting chore — it's the central strategic constraint.
2. **Unit economics.** The observed market price for an ordinary residential yard sign is small ($10–50/month). A 20% fee on $20/month is $4/listing/month. The business only works if it concentrates on *high-value placements* (corner lots, arterial frontage, fences facing traffic, commercial-corridor homes) and *legally clean categories*.

**The reframe that makes it fundable:** Yardtize is not "put any ad in any yard." It is **the compliance-aware marketplace for hyperlocal outdoor advertising** — it knows every city's sign rules, prices every placement with real traffic data, and only offers inventory that can legally exist. The regulation that kills naive competitors becomes the moat.

**The path:** clickable mockup (now) → functional investor demo on real KC maps and real MoDOT/KDOT traffic data (next) → small legal-lane pilot with real homeowners and home-service businesses (then raise).

## 3. Competitive Landscape (what we found)

### Grass Spaces (grass-spaces.com) — the one you spotted
- Live site, positioned as "Rent Your Yard for Ads & Events" — they split focus between ad signs **and** tag-sale/event space rental.
- Essentially zero footprint: only the homepage is indexed by Google, no press, no LinkedIn, no Crunchbase, no app, no social accounts, no discoverable pricing or traction. The site is built in a way that search engines can't read its inner pages.
- No evidence they use traffic data at all — "high-visibility" is asserted, not measured.
- **Takeaway:** validation that others see the idea, but not a meaningful threat today. Yardtize can out-position them on focus (ads only), data (AADT-driven pricing), trust (real leases, deposits), and even basic SEO.
- *Worth doing:* open the site in a normal browser and screenshot their flows — their page content couldn't be read by automated tools.

### At Home Ads (athomeads.com) — the closest direct precedent
- Tennessee startup, running since ~2023: homeowners list free, businesses and political campaigns book placements, hosts keep 100% of fees.
- After 3+ years: roughly **500 Android downloads**. No press, no funding footprint.
- **Takeaway:** the sobering one. Someone has run this exact play for three years without finding liquidity. The lesson is that *supply is easy, demand is hard* — the business lives or dies on getting advertisers, not homeowners.

### Adzookie (2011) — the cautionary tale investors may remember
- Offered to pay mortgages to paint houses as billboards. Got 1,000+ homeowner applications **within hours** — and could fund about a dozen. Dead within the year; ordinances and economics both cited.
- **Takeaway:** homeowner supply is effectively infinite and instant. Never mistake homeowner sign-ups for traction.

### Adjacent proof points
- **Home services already pay for yards informally.** Roofers, solar, lawn care, pest control commonly trade discounts/cash for yard signs. Caution: in roofing this is often a disguised insurance-deductible rebate (illegal in several states) — Yardtize must screen for this, and being the *clean, documented* way to do it is actually a selling point.
- **Real price discovery is modest:** ~$10–20/month offered for bandit-sign placements; $20 for a weekend on a fence at a busy T-intersection (an actual transaction); $60 one-time (AARP's 2006 program); contractor allowances in the low hundreds.
- **Billboard economics for context (AdQuick data):** rural boards $250–$1,500 per 4-week cycle, mid-metro $1,500–$9,000. Pricing = CPM ($6–10 standard) × modeled impressions from traffic counts. Landowners under billboards typically get 15–20% of ad revenue. This is the pricing logic Yardtize borrows, scaled down honestly for sign size.

### Marketplace mechanics to borrow (the Peerspace family)
| Platform | What it rents | Take rate | Lesson for Yardtize |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peerspace | event/production space | 20% host fee + guest processing fee | Host approval of each booking; platform-provided agreement & insurance; polished listing pages |
| Swimply | pools | 20–30% | Trust/insurance justifies the higher take |
| Sniffspot | yards (for dogs!) | 25% | Proof that ordinary yards can be marketplace inventory |
| Neighbor.com | storage/driveways | ~5% host + 8–20% renter fee | Best analogy for *long-duration, low-touch, recurring monthly* rentals — which is what a sign lease is |

**Peerspace is the right skeleton** (Andrew's instinct): browse a map → view a rich listing → request to book with details attached → host approves → platform papers the agreement, takes payment, holds protections, and charges both-sides fees. Yardtize applies that skeleton to a *recurring monthly* rental like Neighbor, with a booking request that carries the sign rendering, duration, and install/decommission election. That's not ripping Peerspace off — it's using the now-standard marketplace pattern on inventory nobody else prices with public traffic data.

## 4. The Legal Reality (read this section twice)

This is what local investors' lawyers will find in one afternoon, so we lead with it and own it.

### The problem
- **Kansas City, MO:** off-premise advertising signs are only allowed within 660 ft of certain highway corridors; outside that, *no outdoor advertising sign is conforming* under current code. Residential zones are categorically out for classic "paid ad for some unrelated business."
- **Olathe, KS:** requires signs to be on-site; residential lots get roughly one 1-sq-ft wall sign plus small temporary signs.
- **The Supreme Court blessed this.** *City of Austin v. Reagan National Advertising* (2022) held that on-premise/off-premise distinctions are content-neutral and constitutional. There is no First Amendment cavalry coming.
- Enforcement is complaint-driven and typically lands on the **homeowner** — which would poison our supply side after the first citation. Add HOA covenants on top for a large share of suburban lots.

### The three legal lanes (this is the strategy)
1. **The small-format temporary-sign lane.** Post-2015 (*Reed v. Town of Gilbert*), many cities rewrote codes to be content-neutral: they regulate size/number/duration, not message. **Overland Park allows any residential lot up to three stake signs totaling 9 sq ft, content unrestricted.** Lenexa allows 6-sq-ft temporary signs up to 90 days. In those cities, a small paid ad sign rotated within duration limits is arguably compliant today. Small-format, high-churn inventory — yard-sign economics, but *legal* yard-sign economics.
2. **The on-premises-nexus lane (the wedge market).** Contractor, builder, and service signs ("roof by X," "lawn treated by Y") are broadly tolerated because the advertised work happened *on that lot*. A marketplace that formalizes and extends this — pays homeowners to host their actual service provider's sign longer, bundles it with the service relationship, documents it cleanly (no deductible-rebate games) — inherits most of that legal comfort. This is also where proven advertiser willingness-to-pay already lives.
3. **The property-quality lane.** Corner lots, homes fronting arterials and commercial corridors, mixed-use frontage, rural highway frontage parcels, and fences facing parking lots or traffic. These placements are worth 10–50× an interior-street yard on traffic, and are disproportionately in more permissive zoning. Fewer, better listings beats thousands of $15/month lawns.

### Compliance as product
The metro has 100+ municipalities across two states with materially different sign codes. Yardtize's answer: a **rules engine keyed to jurisdiction** — drop a pin, and the platform knows the max sign size, count, duration, and setback for that address, and only offers legal inventory. Include: instant-removal guarantee on any citation notice, Yardtize (not the homeowner) absorbs fines, and screening that blocks deductible-rebate abuse. Competitors who ignore this die the Adzookie death; we make it the moat and the pitch.

## 5. Business Model

- **Two-sided fees, Peerspace-style:** host fee ~15–20% of the lease + advertiser service fee ~10%; blended take ~25–30%, justified by compliance, lease, and payment infrastructure (the high-trust end of the take-rate spectrum, like Swimply/Peerspace, not bare matching like Neighbor).
- **Install/decommission upsell:** advertiser chooses self-install (posts a **security deposit** held against proper decommissioning — Andrew's original idea, and it's a good one) or pays for managed install/removal (Yardtize network or the homeowner for a cut).
- **Pricing engine:** suggested rate = f(AADT of fronting road, road class, corner/stoplight adjacency, sign size allowed at that address, seasonality). Owners can override. V1 can be a simple transparent formula — the point is *no competitor prices with data at all*.
- **Lease & payments:** platform-generated lease signed in-app (Docusign API — already connected to this workspace), monthly billing via Stripe, payouts to homeowners, deposit escrow.
- **Honest unit economics:** an ordinary yard at $20/mo yields ~$4–6/mo to Yardtize — that's not a business. A corner-arterial placement at $150–500/mo yields $40–150/mo. The model works on **placement quality and density in one metro**, not national sprawl. Frame the KC pilot accordingly: 100 quality placements at an average $120/mo ≈ $14k GMV/mo ≈ $3.5–4k/mo revenue — small, but a real proof of liquidity that justifies a raise.

## 6. Traffic Data: verified and ready (this part is great news)

The primary value metric is fully buildable in the pilot market:

- **Missouri (MoDOT):** public ArcGIS service with AADT *including city streets and county roads*, 2024 data, confirmed queryable by point location.
  `https://mapping.modot.mo.gov/arcgis/rest/services/BusinessInt/TrafficInfoSegAADT/MapServer` (layers 16–19 = all roads; filter to latest YEAR).
- **Kansas (KDOT):** two public services — state highways plus non-state **arterials and collectors** (2024 data): 
  `https://wfs.ksdot.org/arcgis_web_adaptor/rest/services/Transportation/AADT_Flow_Map/FeatureServer` and `.../AADT_NonState/FeatureServer/0`. Purely residential KS streets aren't covered — but for those, the *nearest classified road* is the honest visibility metric anyway.
- **National backstop (FHWA HPMS):** per-state services covering all 50 states (e.g. `HPMS_FULL_MO_2023`, `HPMS_FULL_KS_2023` at geo.dot.gov) — this is the "and it scales nationally" slide.

**Recommended architecture:** don't hit state servers live per user (KDOT's was flaky in testing). Do an annual bulk load into our own PostGIS database; a dropped pin then gets the nearest 2–3 road segments' AADT in milliseconds, with no dependency on state uptime and no state-line seams. For the very first demo, live queries with caching are acceptable to move fast.

## 7. Build Roadmap

### Phase 0 — Clickable mockup *(now, ~days, built here in Cowork)*
High-fidelity clickable prototype, no real backend: landing page, homeowner flow (address → satellite view → drag pin → traffic count + suggested rate appear → listing preview), business portal (map of listings → listing detail → booking request with rendering upload, duration, install election). Purpose: conversations — homeowners, businesses, potential investors. Cheap to change, fast to show.

### Phase 1 — Functional investor demo *(Claude Code, ~2–4 weeks of iteration)*
The mockup made real: accounts, real Google Maps satellite view, real MoDOT/KDOT AADT lookups, real listings in a database, business portal with inquiry flow. Deployed at yardtize.com. **Cut from this phase:** payments, in-app lease signing, deposits, the full rate engine — those get slides, not code.
- Stack: **Next.js** (app + API), **Supabase** (Postgres + PostGIS + auth), **Google Maps JavaScript API** (satellite tiles, pin placement), **Vercel** (hosting). Chosen for speed, low cost, and being extremely well-trodden ground for AI-assisted building.

### Phase 2 — Legal-lane pilot *(post-first-conversations)*
10–50 real placements in the cleanest jurisdictions (start with Overland Park's 9-sq-ft allowance and on-premises-nexus home-service deals on the Missouri side). Manual lease handling at first, then Docusign integration, then Stripe billing + deposit escrow. This produces the traction slide.

### Phase 3 — Platform depth *(post-raise)*
Rate engine v2, jurisdiction rules engine at scale, install/decommission network, additional metros via HPMS data, HOA tooling, advertiser analytics (impressions reporting from AADT).

## 8. Fundraising Narrative (how the research becomes the pitch)

1. **Market:** OOH advertising is ~$9B/yr in the US and growing; SMBs are largely priced out of it. Eye-level, stoplight-adjacent placements are premium inventory that doesn't exist as a product yet.
2. **Why now / why us:** public traffic data + modern marketplace playbook + a compliance engine nobody has built. Named competitors are weak (show Grass Spaces / At Home Ads) — and their weakness is *exactly* the compliance/data/demand-gen work we're doing.
3. **The honest risk slide:** sign ordinances — and our three legal lanes, instant-takedown guarantee, and jurisdiction rules engine. Investors trust founders who raise the hard issue first.
4. **Wedge:** home-service advertisers (already paying for yards informally) + high-traffic corner/arterial placements in KC. Then metro density, then new metros.
5. **Ask:** funds a KC pilot to prove demand-side liquidity — the one thing no predecessor proved.
